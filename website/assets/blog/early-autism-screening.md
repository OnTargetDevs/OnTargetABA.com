Early autism screening is a standard check given to children to determine if further evaluation for autism is necessary. You may have noticed the term on the recommended list of well checks your pediatrician provides, and you can request a screening for your child at any time. In this article, [On Target ABA](https://ontargetaba.com/), experienced providers of ABA therapy, explain what early autism screening is, when it takes place, and what you can expect if you take your child for autism screening.

## Key Takeaways on Early Autism Screening

- Early autism screening is a brief check that uses questions, observations, or questionnaires to identify signs of autism and determine whether further assessment may be needed.

- Early autism screening can help children access support and interventions sooner, potentially improving developmental and long-term outcomes.

- If a screening suggests possible signs of autism, a healthcare professional may recommend a more comprehensive autism evaluation and discuss appropriate next steps.

- An autism screening is a quick tool administered by a BCBA (Board Certified Behavior Analyst) or pediatrician used to identify potential concerns, while an autism evaluation is a detailed assessment administered by a licensed psychologist, a developmental pediatrician, or a qualified diagnostic specialist used to determine whether a person meets the criteria for an autism diagnosis.

## What Is Early Screening for Autism?

Early autism screening is a quick step used to identify children who may be showing signs of autism spectrum disorder (ASD). It does not provide a diagnosis, but it helps determine whether further evaluation is needed. Pediatricians generally recommend autism screening at 18 and 24 months. Early screening is valuable because it enables earlier intervention, which improves long-term outcomes.

Because brain development happens rapidly in the first few years of life, identifying concerns early can make a significant difference. Early screening helps parents by indicating whether they should look more closely at their child’s development.

## The Importance of Early Screening for Autism

[Research](https://autism.org/earlier-intervention-leads-to-greater-benefits-for-kids-with-autism/) consistently shows that [early intervention](https://ontargetaba.com/early-intervention-autism-program/) improves outcomes for children with autism. Therefore, early autism screening plays a key role in enabling earlier access to support.

The brain is especially adaptable in early childhood. When intervention begins early, children often make stronger gains in:

- Language development

- Social skills

- Adaptive functioning

- Emotional regulation

Even when screening leads to an evaluation that does not result in an autism diagnosis, families still benefit from understanding their child’s developmental profile.

## When Should Autism Screening Take Place?

Most pediatricians follow [national guidelines](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/hcp/diagnosis/screening.html) and recommend both an 18-month autism screening and a 24-month autism screening.

However, autism screening can happen earlier if concerns arise. Parents are often the first to notice subtle signs that something is not quite right. While each child develops at their own pace, persistent differences can signal the need for screening. Importantly, you do not need to wait for a scheduled well-visit if you have concerns. Pediatricians can administer screening tools anytime.

### Signs That May Lead to Autism Screening

Although every child is different, pediatricians often recommend autism screening if any of the following are observed:

- Limited eye contact

- Limited social smiling

- Delayed speech

- Reduced back-and-forth interaction

- Repetitive movements and play

- Intense interest in specific objects

- Sensory sensitivities

Still, it’s important to remember that no single behavior confirms autism. Developmental patterns show us more than isolated behaviors.

If you feel something isn’t quite lining up developmentally, trust that instinct and schedule an autism screening with your pediatrician.

## What Happens at an Autism Screening?

- Autism screening is a short and fairly simple process. It includes: a short questionnaire completed by parents

- Questions about communication, play, and behavior

- Observations by the pediatrician

One commonly used tool is the Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers (M-CHAT). This screening asks about everyday behaviors like pointing, pretend play, and social interaction.

The entire process often takes 10–15 minutes.

If the screening results suggest potential concerns, the pediatrician will usually recommend a comprehensive autism evaluation.

### What If My Child “Fails” an Autism Screening?

If your child ‘fails’ an autism screening, it simply means the results suggest further evaluation is needed.

It does not mean that your child definitely has autism, or that there is no hope.

Some children who screen positive for autism may later receive different developmental diagnoses. Others may qualify for[support services](https://ontargetaba.com/2026/03/04/what-is-an-autism-scholarship-program-asp-and-how-can-it-help-your-child/) even without a formal ASD diagnosis.

### If Autism Screening Confirms the Need for Evaluation

When screening suggests further testing, the next step is a comprehensive autism evaluation performed by a licensed psychologist, a developmental pediatrician, or a qualified diagnostic specialist.

These professionals use standardized assessment tools and structured observation to determine whether a child meets criteria for autism spectrum disorder.

## Autism Screening and Insurance

Many insurance providers require a formal diagnosis before approving services such as ABA therapy. Therefore, screening helps families move toward support services more efficiently.

Taking action early reduces wait times for evaluations and therapy services.

## What Parents Can Do Right Now

If you’ve noticed signs that have you concerned, or simply wish to schedule a routine screening, here are a few practical steps:

1. Schedule a pediatric appointment and request screening.

2. Write down developmental concerns in advance.

3. Record examples of behaviors you’ve observed.

4. Ask about next steps if screening indicates risk.

Additionally, if waitlists for formal evaluations are long, ask your pediatrician about early intervention referrals while waiting.

## Screening vs. Diagnosis: What’s the Difference?

Autism screening is not a diagnosis.

Screening simply indicates whether further evaluation is recommended. A formal autism diagnosis requires an in-depth developmental history, direct intervention with the child, use of standardized assessment tools, and clinical judgment from a qualified professional.

Because screening is only a first step, a positive screen does not automatically mean a child has autism. However, it does mean that moving forward with evaluation is important.

## Frequently Asked Questions on Early Autism Screening

### How early can my child be screened for autism?

A child can be screened for autism as early as 18 months of age. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends autism-specific screening at 18 months and 24 months during routine well-child visits.

### Which questions should I ask my pediatrician about autism?

Ask your pediatrician about developmental milestones, communication skills, social interactions, repetitive behaviors, and next steps if concerns arise. Important questions include: “Does my child show signs of autism?” “Should my child receive a formal evaluation?” “What therapies are available?”, and “How soon should intervention begin?”

### What is the M Chat Questionnaire?

The M-CHAT-R/F (Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers, Revised with Follow-Up) is a screening questionnaire that identifies children at risk for autism spectrum disorder. Parents answer 20 questions about behavior, communication, social interaction, and play. Based on the M-CHAT score, an autism evaluation may be recommended.

### What is the difference between an autism screening and an autism evaluation?

The main difference between an autism screening and an autism evaluation is that a screening identifies whether autism is possible, while an evaluation determines whether autism is present. Autism screenings use brief questionnaires or checklists and typically take 10–30 minutes. Autism evaluations use detailed interviews, observations, developmental history, and standardized assessments, and often take significantly longer.
