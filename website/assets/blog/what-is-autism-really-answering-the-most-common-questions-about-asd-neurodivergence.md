## What Is Autism, Really? Answering the Most Common Questions About ASD & Neurodivergence

If you’ve ever typed “what is autism” into a search bar late at night — after a diagnosis, a teacher’s suggestion, or just a nagging feeling that your child (or you) experiences the world differently — you’re not alone. Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is one of the most talked-about and least understood topics in child development today. Despite growing awareness, misinformation still spreads fast, and real answers can be hard to find in plain language.

This blog is here to change that. Below, we’ve gathered the questions people ask most often about autism and neurodivergence and answered them clearly, compassionately, and without the clinical jargon.

## What Does “Neurodivergent” Actually Mean?

“[Neurodivergent](https://autisticadvocacy.org/)” is an umbrella term describing brains that process, learn, and experience the world differently from what’s considered “typical.” Autism falls under this umbrella, along with ADHD, dyslexia, and other neurological differences.

The key idea behind neurodivergence isn’t that something is “broken” — it’s that human brains naturally vary, the same way height or personality varies. Someone who is neurodivergent isn’t operating with a deficient brain; they’re operating with a *different* one, which comes with its own strengths and challenges.

## What Is Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)?

[Autism spectrum disorder](https://ontargetaba.com/blog/sharing-a-diagnosis-how-to-let-people-know-about-your-childs-autism/) is a developmental condition that affects how a person communicates, interacts socially, and processes sensory information. It’s called a “spectrum” because it looks different in every individual — no two autistic people present exactly the same way.

### Some common (but not universal) traits include:

- Differences in eye contact, facial expression, or conversational back-and-forth

- Strong, focused interests in specific topics

- A preference for routine and predictability

- Sensory sensitivities to sound, light, texture, or touch

- Repetitive movements or behaviors (often called “stimming”)

- Literal interpretation of language or difficulty with social nuance

It’s important to remember: autism is a difference, not an illness. It isn’t something to be “cured,” though many autistic individuals benefit from support that helps them navigate a world built around neurotypical norms.

## What Causes Autism?

This is one of the most searched — and most misunderstood — questions out there. Here’s what current research actually tells us:

- Genetics play a major role. Autism tends to run in families, and researchers have identified numerous genes associated with it.

- It is not caused by vaccines. This myth has been thoroughly debunked by decades of large-scale research, yet it persists online. [Vaccines do not cause autism.](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccine-safety/about/autism.html)

- It is not caused by parenting style. Older, now-discredited theories blamed “cold” parenting for autism. This has no scientific basis.

- Prenatal and environmental factors may contribute in combination with genetic predisposition, though no single environmental cause has been identified.

In short: autism is believed to result from a complex mix of genetic and neurological factors present from early brain development — not from anything a parent did or didn’t do.

## How Is Autism Diagnosed?

### Diagnosis typically involves a comprehensive evaluation by a psychologist, developmental pediatrician, or multidisciplinary team. This usually includes:

1. Developmental history — a detailed look at early milestones and behaviors

2. Direct observation — often using standardized tools like the ADOS-2

3. Caregiver interviews — gathering input from parents or guardians

4. Assessment of communication, social skills, and sensory patterns

### Signs can sometimes be noticed as early as 12–18 months, though many children aren’t diagnosed until later — and a growing number of adults are being diagnosed for the first time well into adulthood. Early identification matters because it opens the door to early intervention, which research consistently links to stronger long-term outcomes.

## Is “High-Functioning Autism” a Real Diagnosis?

Not officially. “High-functioning” and “low-functioning” are informal labels that clinicians have largely moved away from because they oversimplify a complex picture. A person might communicate verbally with ease but struggle significantly with sensory regulation or executive functioning — or vice versa.

Instead, professionals now talk about support needs, which can vary by domain (communication, daily living skills, social interaction) and can even shift day to day. This framing is more accurate and far more respectful of how autism actually shows up in real life.

## What Are the Strengths Associated with Neurodivergence?

Conversations about autism often focus heavily on challenges, but neurodivergent individuals bring real strengths, including:

- Deep focus and expertise in areas of interest

- Pattern recognition and attention to detail

- Honesty and directness in communication

- Strong memory for facts, sequences, or details

- Creative and out-of-the-box problem-solving

Many of the world’s most innovative thinkers, artists, and scientists are believed to be neurodivergent. Recognizing these strengths — not just managing challenges — is a core part of a strengths-based approach to support.

## How Common Is Autism?

[According to the CDC, roughly 1 in 36 children in the U.S](https://abc7news.com/post/estimated-1-31-children-have-autism-diagnosis-rates-increase-us-cdc/16179092/). is identified with autism spectrum disorder. Rates have risen over the past two decades, largely due to broader diagnostic criteria, increased awareness, and better screening — not necessarily a true increase in occurrence.

## What Kind of Support Actually Helps?

Every autistic person’s needs are different, but common forms of support include:

- Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA) therapy — helps build communication, social, and daily living skills through individualized, evidence-based strategies

- Speech and occupational therapy — supports communication and sensory/motor development

- Early intervention programs — provide critical support during key developmental windows

- Social skills groups — build peer interaction in a structured, supportive setting

- Accommodations at school or work — sensory-friendly environments, flexible communication, and predictable routines

The goal of quality support isn’t to make someone “less autistic” — it’s to help them build skills, reduce frustration, and thrive as themselves.

## Final Thoughts

Autism and neurodivergence aren’t things to fear or fix — they’re part of the natural diversity of the human brain. The more we understand ASD, the better equipped we are to support the autistic individuals in our lives, whether that’s a child, a student, a coworker, or ourselves.
