## Autism and Relationships: How to Build Stronger Connections With Your Autistic Child

Every parent wants to feel close to their child. For families raising an autistic child, that closeness is just as possible, but it may look different from what you expected. Understanding autism and relationships starts with one important shift: your child isn’t missing the desire to connect. They may simply connect in their own way, on their own terms, and through their own interests.

At On Target ABA, we work with families across Ohio and Utah who are learning to read their child’s unique ways of showing love, trust, and interest in others. This guide covers what relationships can look like for autistic children, practical ways to strengthen your bond, and how the right support can help relationships grow with siblings, peers, and the wider world.

## Why Connection Can Look Different for Autistic Children

Autism affects how a person communicates, processes sensory information, and experiences social interaction. Those differences shape how relationships form. A child who [avoids eye contact](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5705114/) may still be listening closely. A child who lines up toys beside you instead of playing “with” you may be inviting you into their world in the way that feels safest to them.

Some common differences you might notice include:

- Communication style: Your child may use fewer words, echo phrases (echolalia), use gestures, or rely on a device or picture system to express needs and feelings.

- Eye contact: Many autistic people find eye contact uncomfortable or distracting. Looking away does not mean they aren’t paying attention.

- Shared play: Your child may prefer parallel play, playing near others rather than directly with them, especially in early childhood.

- Sensory needs: Hugs, loud rooms, or busy family gatherings may feel overwhelming, which can affect how comfortable your child is around others.

- Deep interests: Many autistic children have intense passions, and these interests are often the strongest bridge to connection.

Recognizing these differences helps you stop measuring your relationship against a neurotypical checklist and start noticing the connection that’s already there.

## The Parent-Child Bond: Meeting Your Child Where They Are

Your relationship with your child is the foundation for every other relationship they’ll build. The good news is that small, consistent moments of connection add up.

### Learn Your Child’s Language of Affection

Affection doesn’t always look like hugs and “I love you.” For an autistic child, it might look like:

- Bringing you a favorite toy or object to show you

- Sitting near you while doing their own activity

- Repeating a phrase or script you often say together

- Leaning against you or seeking deep pressure

- Sharing facts about something they love

When you start seeing these moments as bids for connection, you can respond warmly and reinforce them.

### Join Their World First

One of the most effective ways to build trust is to step into your child’s interests instead of always pulling them into yours. If your child loves trains, get on the floor and watch the trains with them. Comment on what they’re doing. Imitate their play. Over time, many children begin to welcome you into the activity and even look to you to share the experience.

### Respect Sensory and Physical Boundaries

Forcing hugs, eye contact, or crowded outings can make interactions feel stressful rather than safe. Offering choices, like “Do you want a hug or a high five?”, gives your child control and builds trust. Trust is what relationships are built on.

### Keep Routines Predictable

Predictability helps many autistic children feel secure. Simple rituals, like a bedtime song, a Saturday pancake breakfast, or a specific goodbye phrase, become shared moments your child can count on. These routines often turn into some of the most meaningful connection points in the family.

## Sibling Relationships and Autism

Brothers and sisters of autistic children often become lifelong allies, but the relationship may need some support, especially in the early years.

Siblings might feel confused when their autistic brother or sister doesn’t want to play the same way, or frustrated when routines or meltdowns change family plans. Here’s how parents can help:

1. Explain autism in age-appropriate terms. Simple, positive language works best, such as “Her brain works differently, so she likes to play in her own way.”

2. Find shared activities. Swinging, bubbles, water play, building blocks, or music are often easy entry points for both kids.

3. Carve out one-on-one time for every child. Siblings need their own time with you, too.

4. Give siblings a role, not a job. Let them help model play or teach a game, but avoid making them responsible for caregiving.

5. Celebrate small wins together. When siblings share a laugh or take turns, notice it and praise it.

## Friendships and Peer Relationships

Many autistic children want friends but find the unwritten rules of social interaction confusing. Turn-taking, joining a group game, reading facial expressions, and handling changes in play can all feel unpredictable.

Friendship skills can be learned and practiced, just like any other skill. Helpful approaches include:

- Structured play dates: Short, planned activities with one peer work better than open-ended, unstructured time.

- Interest-based groups: Clubs or activities built around your child’s passions, like Lego, animals, or art, give kids a natural topic to share.

- Social stories and visual supports: These can help children understand what to expect in new social situations.

- Practice with trusted adults first: Role-playing greetings, sharing, and turn-taking at home builds confidence before trying them with peers.

It’s also worth remembering that friendship doesn’t have to look a certain way. Some autistic children thrive with one close friend. Others enjoy friendships built around a shared activity more than conversation. What matters is that the relationship feels good to your child.

## Common Myths About Autism and Relationships

Misconceptions can make families feel discouraged. Let’s clear up a few:

- Myth: Autistic children don’t want relationships. Most autistic children do want connection; they may just express it differently.

- Myth: [Autistic people lack empathy.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/254245139_On_the_ontological_status_of_autism_The_%27double_empathy_problem%27) Many autistic people feel emotions deeply. The difference is often in how emotions are expressed or read, not whether they’re felt.

- Myth: No eye contact means no attention. For many autistic people, avoiding eye contact actually helps them listen and focus.

- Myth: Social skills can’t improve. With the right support, children can build communication and social skills at their own pace.

## How ABA Therapy Can Support Relationships

Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA) therapy, when it’s individualized, play-based, and focused on your child’s quality of life, can help build the skills that make relationships easier and more rewarding.

At On Target ABA, relationship-related goals may include:

- Functional communication: Helping your child express wants, needs, and feelings through words, signs, pictures, or devices, which reduces frustration on both sides.

- Joint attention: Building the ability to share focus on an object or activity with another person, a key foundation for connection.

- Play skills: Expanding play from solo activities to parallel and then interactive play with siblings and peers.

- Emotional regulation: Supporting your child in managing big feelings, which makes family time and social situations less stressful.

- Parent training: Coaching caregivers with strategies to use at home so progress carries over into everyday family life.

## A Note on the Research

Much of the research on[autism and relationships](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1362361312467866) has historically focused on boys and relied heavily on parent or clinician reports rather than the perspectives of autistic people themselves. Girls, nonspeaking children, and children from diverse cultural backgrounds are underrepresented in many studies. That’s one more reason every child’s relationship journey should be treated as individual, not measured against averages.

### Practical Tips You Can Start Today

If you’re looking for easy ways to strengthen your connection this week, try these:

1. Spend 10 minutes a day playing your child’s way, following their lead with no demands.

2. Narrate what they’re doing in a warm, simple voice instead of asking lots of questions.

3. Notice and respond to small bids for attention, like showing you an object.

4. Offer choices in physical affection and respect their answers.

5. Create one simple, predictable family ritual.

6. Celebrate progress, however small it seems.

## Building Connection, One Moment at a Time

Autism and relationships are not a contradiction. Your autistic child can form deep, meaningful bonds with you, their siblings, and their friends. The path may look different, and that’s okay. When families learn to recognize their child’s unique ways of connecting and get the right support, relationships have room to flourish.
