## Co-Occurring Conditions in Autism: The Truth About Diagnostic Overshadowing

When a child receives a diagnosis of Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), families naturally focus all of their attention on understanding the unique behaviors, sensory differences, and communication styles associated with the spectrum. For a long time, clinical frameworks treated ASD as a singular, isolated bubble. However, updated pediatric research tells a vastly different story.

The reality is that it is incredibly rare for autism to exist entirely on its own. Instead, the vast majority of individuals on the autism spectrum navigate at least one—and often multiple—co-occurring neurodevelopmental or mental health conditions.

When clinical teams look solely at a single diagnosis checklist, they risk missing secondary hurdles that fundamentally impact a child’s development. Understanding these intersecting layers is the key to creating holistic, effective behavioral support.

## What Does the Data Tell Us? The Prevalence of Co-Occurring Conditions

According to large-scale data compiled across the [Autism Research Institute (ARI) Mental Health Resource Hub](https://autism.org/autism-mental-health/) and the National Institutes of Health (NIH), approximately 78% to 91% of children and adolescents on the autism spectrum meet the criteria for at least one co-occurring psychiatric or developmental diagnosis. Furthermore, nearly half of those individuals actively manage two or more secondary conditions.

Rather than being separate, unrelated issues, these overlapping conditions interact dynamically with a person’s underlying sensory and processing baselines. Let’s break down the three most common overlaps seen in clinical practice:

## 1. The ADHD Overlap (AuDHD)

For decades, clinical manuals strictly forbade clinicians from diagnosing a child with both Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) and ASD. That rule has thankfully changed. Statistical registries monitored by the [Autism Speaks Medical Conditions Guide](https://www.autismspeaks.org/medical-conditions-associated-autism) indicate that between 20% and 50% of individuals on the spectrum also meet the full criteria for ADHD.

When these conditions co-occur, executive functioning challenges are heavily compounded. A child might struggle intensely with:

- Shifting focus smoothly between activities (attention disengagement).

- Working memory limitations.

- Restlessness and psychomotor agitation.

## 2. Chronic Social Anxiety

Clinical data outlines that up to 50% of individuals on the spectrum battle significant, clinical anxiety disorders. Anxiety in neurodivergent individuals rarely looks identical to anxiety in neurotypical peers. It is deeply driven by environmental overstimulation, changes in daily structure, and the baseline stress of navigating environments that are not optimized for intense sensory profiles.

## 3. Depression and Neurodivergent Burnout

Depression rates scale upwards significantly as individuals move into adolescence and early adulthood. Clinical tracking highlights that the chronic exertion needed to navigate daily peer interactions can spark deep depressive episodes. In individuals on the spectrum, depression often manifests uniquely—such as a sudden, sharp withdrawal from a long-term “special interest” or a steep increase in sleep disruptions.

## The Hidden Barrier: Understanding Social “Masking”

One of the biggest reasons secondary conditions like anxiety and depression go untreated for years is a behavioral copy mechanism known as social masking.

Masking occurs when an individual consciously or unconsciously suppresses their natural characteristics—such as hiding repetitive self-soothing behaviors (stimming), forcing uncomfortable eye contact, or copying peer facial expressions—simply to blend into neurotypical school or community environments.

While masking might make a child appear completely calm, compliant, and successful on the outside, it comes at a massive physiological cost. Forcing the nervous system to perform unnatural social scripts causes a severe internal accumulation of stress. Over time, this constant suppression results in severe anxiety, emotional exhaustion, and eventual neurodivergent burnout.

## What is Diagnostic Overshadowing?


When a child has multiple overlapping traits, families frequently run into a clinical phenomenon known as diagnostic overshadowing.

Diagnostic overshadowing occurs when a practitioner, educator, or caregiver attributes *all* of a child’s behavioral challenges exclusively to their primary autism diagnosis. For example, if a child is highly hyperactive, impulsive, or struggling to complete tasks, a provider might say, *“That is just a part of their autism,”* completely missing the fact that the child has co-occurring, [treatable ADHD.](https://ontargetaba.com/2026/04/30/autism-and-mental-health-understanding-co-occurring-conditions-and-how-to-get-support/)

Overshadowing delays crucial support. When a secondary condition is masked by a primary label, families lose access to targeted interventions that could dramatically improve their child’s day-to-day quality of life.
