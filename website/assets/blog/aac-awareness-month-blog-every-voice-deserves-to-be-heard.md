## October Is AAC Awareness Month: Why Every Voice Matters

October is AAC Awareness Month, a time to celebrate the many ways people communicate and to push for wider access to the tools that make communication possible. For millions of children and adults, talking is not the only way to say “I want,” “I’m done,” “that’s funny” or “I love you.” A child might tap a picture on a tablet, point to a letter board, sign with their hands or press a big button that speaks for them.

Every one of those messages is real communication. AAC Awareness Month exists because too many people still see these tools as a last resort, a crutch or something to try only after speech has failed. The truth is the opposite: communication is a basic human right, and AAC is one of the most powerful ways to protect it.

At OnTarget ABA, we work with children and families across Ohio and Utah every day. We have seen what happens when a child who has been frustrated, misunderstood or shut out finally gets a way to be heard. This guide explains what AAC is, clears up the most common myths, and shares simple ways you can advocate for AAC this month and all year long.

## What Is AAC?

[AAC stands for Augmentative and Alternative Communication](https://aacworkshop.pbworks.com/f/AAC05EarlyIntMythsRealitiesRomskiSevcik.pdf). It covers any method that adds to (augments) or replaces (alternative) spoken language. Most of us already use some AAC without thinking about it: a wave, a thumbs-up or a text message all communicate without speech.

AAC is usually grouped into two broad types.

Unaided AAC uses only the body:

- Sign language, such as American Sign Language (ASL)

- Gestures, like pointing, waving or nodding

- Facial expressions and body language

Aided AAC uses a tool or device, ranging from low-tech to high-tech:

- Low-tech: picture cards, Picture Exchange Communication System [(PECS)](https://ontargetaba.com/blog/pecs-for-autism-a-parents-guide-to-the-picture-exchange-communication-system/) books, visual schedules, first/then boards, alphabet and letter boards

- Mid-tech: simple recorded-message buttons and switches that say one word or phrase, like “more” or “help”

- High-tech: speech-generating devices and tablet apps with large vocabularies, grids of symbols and the ability to build full sentences

No single system is “best.” Many people use a mix, signing at the dinner table, using a device at school and pointing to pictures at the park. [The right AAC is whatever lets a person say what they want to say,](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1092-4388(2006/021)) to whoever they want to say it to, wherever they are.

## Who Uses AAC?

AAC is used by people of every age, with many different needs. Some use it every day for life; others use it for a season while speech develops or recovers. People who may benefit from AAC include:

- Autistic children and adults, including those who speak some words but not reliably or under stress

- Children with apraxia of speech, Down syndrome, cerebral palsy or other developmental differences

- People with hearing differences

- Adults after a stroke, brain injury or with progressive conditions such as ALS

- Anyone who has a temporary loss of speech, such as after surgery

For autistic children in particular, speech can be inconsistent. A child might talk easily at home but go quiet in a busy classroom, or have words for favorite topics but not for pain, hunger or fear. AAC gives them a reliable back-up so the message gets through no matter how the day is going.

There is no minimum age, IQ score or set of “prerequisite skills” required to start. If a person’s communication needs are not fully met by speech alone, AAC is worth exploring.

## Common AAC Myths, and the Facts


Misunderstandings about AAC are one of the biggest barriers to access. Here are the myths families hear most often.

Myth 1: “AAC will stop my child from learning to talk.” Fact: Research reviews have found that AAC does not hinder speech development, and in many studies children’s spoken words stayed the same or increased after AAC was introduced. AAC takes the pressure off and models language, which can make talking easier, not harder.

Myth 2: “AAC is a last resort.” Fact: AAC works best when it is introduced early. Waiting until other approaches “fail” means months or years of lost communication, frustration and missed learning.

Myth 3: “My child is too young or not ready.” Fact: There are no prerequisites. Toddlers can use simple picture systems, and devices can grow in complexity as the child grows.

Myth 4: “AAC is only for people who can’t speak at all.” Fact: Many AAC users speak some words. AAC fills the gaps, helping with harder messages, new settings or tough moments.

Myth 5: “Once you have a device, the work is done.” Fact: A device is just the start. Children learn AAC the same way they learn speech: by seeing it used around them, over and over, in real situations.

*A note on the research:* much of the published AAC research involves small groups of participants, and many studies focus on young autistic children or children with developmental disabilities. Findings for teens, adults and other populations are more limited, so individual assessment by a qualified professional, such as a speech-language pathologist, remains essential.

## How to Advocate for AAC


Advocacy does not require a title or a microphone. It starts with how we treat AAC users and the people around them. Here is how different groups can help.

### For parents and caregivers:

1. Ask about AAC early. If your child’s communication needs aren’t fully met, request an AAC evaluation from a speech-language pathologist.

2. Model, model, model. Use your child’s AAC yourself during play, meals and routines, without expecting them to copy you right away.

3. Make AAC available all day. A device left in a backpack can’t be used. Keep it charged, close by and part of daily life.

4. Put AAC in your child’s plan. Ask for AAC goals and supports in their IEP, therapy plan and daily schedule.

### For teachers and schools:

- Give every staff member basic training on the student’s system

- Add core words and symbols to classroom displays

- Allow extra wait time so AAC users can finish their message

- Treat AAC responses as equal to spoken ones in class discussions

### For the community:

- Speak to the AAC user directly, not to the person beside them
Be patient, and never finish their sentence or touch their device without permission
Choose businesses, events and playgrounds that offer communication boards and visual supports
Share accurate information, like this post, to help retire old myths

Small habits like these send a big message: your voice counts here.

## Every Voice Deserves to Be Heard


AAC Awareness Month is a reminder that communication is not about how a message is delivered. It is about being understood. A tap, a sign, a picture or a spoken word all carry the same weight when they come from someone with something to say.

This October, take one small step: learn a few signs, model on a device, share this post or ask your child’s team about AAC. Every bit of awareness helps build a world where every person has a voice.
