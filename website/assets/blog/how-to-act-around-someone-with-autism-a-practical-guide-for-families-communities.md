## How to Act With People With Autism: A Practical Guide

[Autism spectrum disorder (ASD)](https://ontargetaba.com/blog/asd-inclusive-practices-supporting-autism-neurodivergence/) affects how a person communicates, processes sensory input, and interacts socially — but it does not define who they are. Whether you’re a parent, teacher, coworker, neighbor, or simply someone who wants to be a better ally, learning how to act with people with autism starts with one core idea: every autistic person is different. There is no single “right” way to interact that applies to everyone, [but there are respectful, evidence-informed practices that make interactions smoother,](https://www.sf.gov/information--tips-communicating-people-autism-spectrum) safer, and more comfortable for everyone involved.

At On Target ABA, we work with children and families across Ohio and Utah every day, and we’ve seen firsthand how small shifts in approach can make a big difference in how included and understood an autistic person feels. This guide breaks down the practical, everyday behaviors that help you connect more effectively.

## Why Understanding Autism Matters

Autism is a spectrum, which means presentations vary widely. Some autistic individuals are highly verbal; others communicate through alternative methods like [AAC (augmentative and alternative communication)](https://ontargetaba.com/blog/communication-in-asd-a-parents-guide-to-unlocking-your-childs-voice/) devices, sign language, or gestures. Some seek social interaction eagerly, while others need more space and time to engage. Sensory processing also varies significantly — sounds, lights, textures, or smells that seem unremarkable to one person can be overwhelming to another.

Because of this variability, the most important skill isn’t memorizing a script — it’s developing flexibility, patience, and a willingness to follow the individual’s lead.

## Core Principles for Interacting With Autistic Individuals

### 1. Presume Competence

[Speak to autistic individuals](https://www.actionbehavior.com/resource/post/9-tips-for-communicating-with-children-diagnosed-with-autism) — regardless of age or communication style — with the same respect you’d offer anyone else. Avoid talking about them as if they aren’t present, and don’t assume that limited verbal communication reflects limited understanding.

### 2. Communicate Clearly and Directly

Many autistic people process language literally and may find sarcasm, idioms, or vague instructions confusing. Helpful habits include:

- Using clear, concrete language instead of figures of speech

- Giving one instruction at a time rather than multi-step requests

- Allowing extra processing time before expecting a response

- Confirming understanding gently, without pressure

### 3. Respect Sensory Needs

Sensory sensitivities are common in autism. Bright lights, loud noises, strong smells, or unexpected touch can cause real distress. When interacting with someone who is autistic:

- Ask before touching them, including handshakes or hugs

- Be mindful of volume and background noise in shared spaces

- Allow the use of sensory tools (headphones, fidgets, sunglasses) without comment

- Avoid forcing eye contact — many autistic individuals process information better while looking away

### 4. Support Stimming, Don’t Suppress It

“Stimming” — repetitive movements or sounds like hand-flapping, rocking, or humming — is a natural self-regulation strategy for many autistic people. Unless a behavior is unsafe, there’s no need to stop or discourage it. Stimming helps manage stress, sensory overload, or excitement, and interrupting it can increase anxiety rather than reduce it.

### 5. Be Patient With Social Differences

Eye contact, small talk, and typical conversational turn-taking may not come naturally to an autistic person, and that’s okay. Some may:

- Prefer to discuss specific interests in depth rather than making casual small talk

- Need more time to respond in conversation

- Miss subtle social cues like sarcasm, facial expressions, or tone shifts

- Communicate honestly and directly, sometimes without social “softening”

None of these differences reflect rudeness or disinterest — they reflect a different way of processing social information.

### 6. Offer Predictability

Unexpected changes in routine or environment can be genuinely distressing for many autistic individuals. When possible:

- Give advance notice of changes in plans

- Use visual schedules or clear verbal walkthroughs of what to expect

- Avoid surprises, even well-intentioned ones (like surprise parties)

- Allow transition time between activities

## What to Avoid

Just as important as knowing what to do is knowing what not to do. Common missteps include:

- Speaking in a “baby voice” or overly simplified tone to adults or older children — this can feel condescending

- Forcing eye contact or physical affection — always let the person set the pace

- Interrupting stimming behaviors unless there’s a safety concern

- Assuming nonverbal means non-thinking — many nonverbal autistic individuals have rich inner worlds and full comprehension

- Using functioning labels like “high-functioning” or “low-functioning” — these labels oversimplify a complex spectrum and can be dismissive of real support needs

- Speaking over or on behalf of an autistic person when they are capable of communicating (even if support is needed)

## Tips for Specific Settings

### At School or in the Classroom 

### Teachers and classmates can support autistic students by offering structured routines, quiet spaces for sensory breaks, and clear, literal instructions. Peer education about neurodiversity also helps reduce stigma and build genuine friendships.

### In the Workplace 

### Autistic employees benefit from clear written instructions, predictable schedules, and direct communication about expectations. Employers should be open to reasonable accommodations, such as noise-canceling headphones, flexible lighting, or written follow-ups after verbal meetings.

### In Public or Social Settings

### If you encounter someone who appears to be having a sensory overload moment (sometimes called a meltdown), the most helpful response is usually to reduce stimulation — lower your voice, give space, and avoid crowding them. Avoid judgment; what looks like a “tantrum” is often a genuine physiological response to overwhelm, not defiance.

As a Parent or Caregiver 

### Parents raising an autistic child benefit from working closely with professionals — such as Board Certified Behavior Analysts (BCBAs) and Registered Behavior Technicians (RBTs) — who can help build individualized strategies for communication, skill-building, and behavior support. At On Target ABA, our center-based, home-based, and school-based programs are designed around each child’s unique strengths and needs, not a one-size-fits-all model.

## A Few Golden Rules

If you remember nothing else, these three principles will serve you well in almost any interaction:

1. Ask, don’t assume. When in doubt about communication style, sensory needs, or preferences, ask the individual (or their support person) directly.

2. Follow their lead. Let the autistic person set the pace for conversation, physical contact, and social engagement.

3. Treat them as a whole person. Autism is one part of someone’s identity — not the entirety of who they are.

## Final Thoughts

Learning how to act with people with autism isn’t about following a rigid checklist — it’s about approaching every interaction with curiosity, patience, and respect for individual differences. Small adjustments in communication style, sensory awareness, and expectations can transform an interaction from stressful to genuinely connecting.
