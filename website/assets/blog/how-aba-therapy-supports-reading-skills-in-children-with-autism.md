## How ABA Therapy Supports Reading Skills in Children with Autism

Reading is one of the most important skills a child develops — it opens the door to communication, independence, academic success, and lifelong learning. For many children with autism, however, reading doesn’t come easily. Differences in attention, language processing, motivation, and social communication can make traditional reading instruction feel out of reach.

That’s where Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA) therapy comes in. ABA doesn’t just address behavior — it’s also a powerful, evidence-based framework for teaching academic and pre-academic skills, including reading. At On Target ABA, our clinicians integrate literacy goals into individualized treatment plans, helping children build reading skills in a way that’s structured, motivating, and tailored to how they learn best.

In this post, we’ll explore why reading can be difficult for children with autism, how ABA therapy breaks the reading process into achievable steps, and what parents can do to reinforce these skills outside of therapy sessions.

## Why Reading Can Be Challenging for Children with Autism

Every child with autism is different, and reading difficulties can stem from a variety of underlying factors. Some common challenges include:

Instead of waiting for your child to tell you they feel sick, it helps to watch for behavioral and physical cues, such as:

- Language delays — Reading relies heavily on vocabulary and language comprehension, which may develop more slowly in children with autism.

- Attention and focus differences — Sustained attention to a book or task can be difficult, especially without built-in motivation.

- Difficulty with abstract concepts — Letters representing sounds, and sounds combining into words, is an abstract skill that some children need explicitly taught rather than intuitively absorbed.

- [Hyperlexia](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/assessment-research/hyperlexia-in-children-with-autism-spectrum-disorders/)— Interestingly, some children with autism can decode words very early (sometimes before age 3) but struggle significantly with comprehension — understanding what they’ve read.

- Social communication differences — Reading comprehension, especially of stories involving characters’ emotions or intentions, can be harder for children who experience the world differently in social contexts.

It’s worth noting that research on reading and autism does skew toward certain profiles — much of the literature focuses on hyperlexia and comprehension gaps, with less coverage of children who have significant language delays alongside emerging literacy goals. This is exactly why an individualized approach matters so much.

## How ABA Therapy Approaches Reading Instruction

ABA therapy is built on the principle of breaking complex skills into small, teachable steps and reinforcing progress along the way. Reading is no exception. Rather than assuming a child will pick up literacy skills through general classroom exposure, ABA therapists assess exactly where a child is starting from and build a personalized instructional plan.

### 1. Assessing the Child’s Current Skill Level

[Before any reading goals are introduced, a Board Certified Behavior Analyst (BCBA) conducts an assessment](https://researchautism.org/oaracle-newsletter/evaluating-a-reading-program-with-aba-components/) to determine the child’s current abilities. This might include:

- Letter and sound recognition

- Phonemic awareness (the ability to hear and manipulate sounds in words)

- Sight word recognition

- Comprehension of simple stories or instructions

- Attention span and motivation for book-related activities

This baseline helps the team identify whether a child needs to start with foundational pre-reading skills or is ready for more advanced literacy work.

### 2. Building Foundational Pre-Reading Skills

For children who aren’t yet reading, ABA therapy often starts with foundational skills that support later literacy, such as:

- Matching and sorting — Matching identical letters or pictures builds visual discrimination skills.

- Receptive identification — Teaching a child to point to or select a specific letter or word when named.

- Expressive labeling — Teaching a child to name letters, sounds, or words independently.

- Joint attention with books — Building the ability to sit, attend to, and engage with a book alongside an adult, which is foundational for shared reading experiences.

These skills are taught using discrete trial training ([DTT](https://ontargetaba.com/blog/what-is-discrete-trial-training-dtt-a-complete-guide-for-autism-families/)), natural environment teaching (NET), or a blend of both, depending on what works best for the individual child.

### 3. Teaching Phonics and Decoding

Once foundational skills are in place, therapists move into phonics-based instruction — teaching children to connect letters with sounds and blend those sounds into words. ABA therapists often use:

- Errorless learning techniques to minimize frustration and build confidence early on

- Prompting and fading strategies, gradually reducing support as the child becomes more independent

- [Reinforcement systems](https://ontargetaba.com/blog/the-power-of-reinforcement-how-positive-aba-strategies-motivate-real-change/) tailored to what motivates the individual child, whether that’s praise, tokens, or preferred activities

### 4. Building Sight Word Vocabulary

Many children benefit from learning high-frequency “sight words” — common words that appear often in text but may not follow typical phonetic rules (like “the,” “was,” or “said”). ABA therapists use repeated practice, flashcards, matching games, and reinforcement to help these words become automatic.

### 5. Developing Reading Comprehension

For children who can decode words but struggle to understand what they’ve read — a common pattern seen in hyperlexia — [ABA therapy shifts focus toward comprehension-building strategies](https://www.advancedautism.com/post/improving-reading-comprehension-through-aba-based-academic-support), such as:

- Asking “wh” questions (who, what, where, when, why) about a story

- Sequencing story events in order

- Identifying characters’ feelings and motivations

- Making predictions about what might happen next

- Connecting story content to the child’s own experiences

This is often one of the more nuanced areas of ABA-based literacy instruction, since comprehension involves not just language skills but perspective-taking and inferencing — areas that may need additional, explicit teaching for children with autism.

## Why Individualization Matters So Much in Reading Instruction

No two children learn to read the same way, and this is especially true for children with autism. A child with hyperlexia who decodes fluently but struggles with comprehension needs a very different intervention plan than a child who is just learning to recognize letters. ABA therapy’s strength lies in its flexibility — goals are continuously reassessed and adjusted based on data collected during sessions, ensuring that instruction always matches the child’s current needs.

This data-driven approach also means progress is measurable. Parents and caregivers can see, session by session, exactly which skills are improving and where additional support may be needed — rather than relying on guesswork.

## How Parents Can Support Reading Skills at Home

### ABA-based reading instruction doesn’t have to stay confined to therapy sessions. Here are a few ways parents can reinforce literacy skills at home:

- Make reading a routine. Consistent daily reading time, even just 10–15 minutes, builds familiarity and comfort with books.

- Choose high-interest books. Books related to a child’s special interests can dramatically increase engagement and motivation.

- Point and label. While reading together, point to words and pictures, naming them aloud to reinforce vocabulary.

- Ask simple questions. Pause during a story to ask what just happened or what might happen next — this builds comprehension skills naturally.

- Celebrate small wins. Positive reinforcement for effort and progress — not just correct answers — helps build a positive association with reading.

- Coordinate with your child’s BCBA. Ask your child’s therapy team for specific strategies or materials that align with what’s being worked on in sessions, so home and therapy efforts reinforce each other.

## Final Thoughts

Reading is a foundational life skill, and for children with autism, it often requires a thoughtful, individualized approach to unlock. ABA therapy offers exactly that — a structured, evidence-based method for breaking literacy down into achievable steps, building motivation along the way, and adjusting instruction based on real data and real progress.
